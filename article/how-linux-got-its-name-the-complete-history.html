How Linux Got Its Name: The Complete History

The name "Linux" is universally recognized today, but it was not the original name intended by its creator, Linus Torvalds. In 1991, Torvalds developed a free operating system kernel and planned to name it "Freax," viewing the name "Linux" as too egotistical. However, the intervention of a system administrator hosting the project's files permanently cemented the name "Linux," setting the stage for one of the most significant open-source software movements in computing history.

In the early stages of development, Linus Torvalds, a computer science student at the University of Helsinki, used the working title "Linux" on his private machine as a simple portmanteau of his first name and "Minix," the educational Unix-like system he aimed to replace. Despite this initial moniker, Torvalds considered using his own name for a public release self-centered and unsuitable.

Instead, Torvalds coined the official project name "Freax." This name combined the words "free," "freak," and the letter "x," which was widely used at the time to denote Unix-like operating systems. Torvalds used the name Freax for about half a year, storing the project's source code and development files under this label.

The shift to "Linux" occurred in September 1991 when the operating system was prepared for public distribution. Ari Lemmke, a volunteer administrator at the Helsinki University of Technology who managed the project's download server at the Finnish University and Research Network (FUNET), disliked the name Freax. Believing it was unappealing and not professional enough, Lemmke independently named the project's FTP directory /pub/OS/Linux without consulting Torvalds beforehand.

Faced with the upload already published under the name Linux, Torvalds decided to accept the change. The community quickly embraced the name, and Torvalds officially adopted it for subsequent versions of the kernel.

Later, as the kernel was combined with userland utilities and compilers developed by the Free Software Foundation, a debate arose regarding the naming of the complete operating system. Richard Stallman and the Free Software Foundation advocated for the term "GNU/Linux" to acknowledge that the system paired the GNU software suite with the Linux kernel. While many distributions and technical documents use "GNU/Linux," the simple name "Linux"—first popularized by Ari Lemmke’s FTP directory—remains the widespread standard used by the general public.
